You land a beautiful yellowfin, get it home, cut into the loin and find pale, soft, grayish meat that smells a little sour. That's tuna burn, and it's one of the most frustrating ways to lose a good fish. The meat is safe to eat, but it's mushy, bland and nowhere near sushi grade.

The good news: tuna burn is almost entirely about how the fish is handled in the first hour after it's hooked. Here's what causes it and how to prevent it.

What is tuna burn?

Tuna burn (the Japanese call it yake, which means "burned") is a breakdown of the muscle that leaves the meat:

  • Pale or gray instead of deep red or pink
  • Soft, mushy or grainy instead of firm
  • Sour or metallic tasting
  • Watery, with the loin often falling apart when you cut it

It usually shows up worst near the backbone, in the thickest part of the loin, because that's where the fish stays hottest the longest.

What causes tuna burn?

Tuna aren't like most fish. They can keep their muscles warmer than the water around them, and a hard-fighting tuna gets warmer still. Burn happens when that heat combines with stress:

  1. A long, hard fight burns through the fish's energy and floods the muscle with lactic acid.
  2. Thrashing on deck adds more stress, more heat and bruising.
  3. The core stays hot. If the fish isn't chilled fast, the heat trapped deep in the loin plus the acid starts breaking the muscle down from the inside.

The result: the fish essentially starts to cook and spoil from the inside before you ever get it home. Big fish, warm water and long fights make it more likely.

How to prevent tuna burn: 6 steps

1. Keep the fight short

Use tackle heavy enough to land the fish in a reasonable time. A 45-minute fight on light gear produces a hot, exhausted, acid-loaded fish. You can't always control this, but don't play a fish longer than you need to.

2. Land it cleanly

Gaff the fish in the head or shoulder area, not the loin, and get it on deck in one motion. A gaff that flexes with head shakes is less likely to tear out and turn a clean landing into a fight at the boat. See our gaff size guide for the right length and hook for tuna.

3. Kill it immediately with a brain spike

This is the single biggest step. Every second a tuna thrashes on deck adds heat and acid. A tuna spike pushed into the brain (through the soft spot on top of the head) stops the fish instantly. This is the Japanese ike jime method; our ike jime guide walks through it step by step.

4. Bleed it right away

While the heart is still beating, cut behind the pectoral fins and/or through the gills and let the fish bleed head-down in seawater. Blood left in the meat speeds up spoilage and darkens the flesh. Full details in how to bleed a fish.

5. Get it into an ice slurry, fast

This is where most burn is prevented or lost. Plain ice cubes only touch the fish in a few spots. An ice slurry (crushed ice mixed with seawater until it's slushy) surrounds the whole fish and pulls heat out much faster.

  • Bring more ice than you think you need. A rough rule many crews use is about as much ice by weight as fish.
  • Use seawater, not fresh water. Salt water gets colder than fresh without freezing.
  • Keep the slurry topped up. As the ice melts, add more.

6. Cool the core, not just the skin

On big tuna, the outside can feel cold while the backbone is still warm. To get cold into the middle:

  • Gill and gut the fish once it's bled, and pack ice into the belly cavity.
  • Keep it out of the sun at every step, including while it bleeds.
  • Don't stack warm fish on top of each other in the slurry.

Can you still eat burned tuna?

Usually, yes. Tuna burn affects quality, not safety, as long as the fish was kept cold afterward. Burned meat isn't great raw, but it works fine cooked: tuna salad, fish tacos, smoked tuna dip or anything with a marinade. Spoiled fish is a different thing. If it smells strongly of ammonia or is slimy, throw it out.

FAQ

What does tuna burn look like?
Pale, grayish or "cooked-looking" meat that's soft and watery, usually worst near the backbone.

Does bleeding a tuna prevent burn?
Bleeding helps, but on its own it isn't enough. The biggest factors are a quick kill and fast, deep chilling. Bleeding works best combined with both.

What is an ice slurry?
Crushed ice mixed with seawater until it's slushy. It surrounds the whole fish and chills it much faster than ice cubes alone.
