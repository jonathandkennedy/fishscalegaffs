Most of us were taught to land a fish, toss it in the cooler and keep fishing. It's how it's always been done. But if you've ever opened the lid an hour later and seen a fish still gasping, you've probably wondered whether there's a better way.

There is. This post looks at what the science actually says about how fish die, which methods are humane and which aren't, and why a tuna spike (the Japanese ike jime method) is one of the best options, for the fish and for the meat.

Quick answer

The most humane way to kill a fish is to destroy the brain immediately, either with a brain spike or with a hard, accurate blow to the head. Both make the fish unconscious almost instantly when done correctly. The least humane methods, and unfortunately the most common, are letting the fish suffocate in air or in a cooler, or putting it on ice while it's still alive.

Do fish feel pain?

For a long time the common answer was "not really." The research has moved on. Since the early 2000s, studies on trout and other species have found that fish have pain receptors, react to painful stimuli in ways that go beyond simple reflex, and change their behavior after being hurt. Scientists still debate exactly what a fish experiences, but most welfare bodies now treat fish as animals that can suffer, and recommend killing them quickly.

You don't have to settle the philosophy to make a practical call. If there's a reasonable chance a fish can suffer, and a fast kill costs you two seconds, there's no good reason to let it die slowly.

What a slow death actually looks like

When a fish is left in air, its gills collapse and it can't take in oxygen. It doesn't die right away. It slowly suffocates, and it can stay conscious for a long time while that happens.

  • A 2025 study in Scientific Reports on rainbow trout estimated it can take roughly 2 to 25 minutes for a trout to lose consciousness in air, with an average of around 10 minutes of moderate to severe pain per fish.
  • The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) has noted that some hardy species, like carp, can survive hours out of water.
  • Bigger, stronger fish often thrash hard the whole time, which is bad for the fish and bad for you when there are hooks and spines flying around the deck.

Why a brain spike works

A fish's brain is small and sits just behind and above the eyes. A properly placed spike goes straight into it. Instead of trying to knock the fish out, you physically destroy the part that's doing the experiencing. When it's done right:

  • The fish goes limp almost instantly. You'll usually see a shudder, the fins flare, and sometimes a flash of color, then nothing.
  • There's no second chance needed. Unlike a blow to the head, where a glancing hit just stuns, a spike in the brain doesn't wear off.
  • The heart keeps beating for a few minutes, so the fish bleeds out much better. That's why ike jime fish are prized by sushi chefs.
  • No thrashing means less bruising and less lactic acid, so the meat stays firmer, cleaner and fresher. On tuna, it's one of the best defenses against tuna burn.

Welfare reviews of wild-caught fish have reached the same conclusion: stunning or killing fish so they're immediately insensible improves their welfare at the time of death, and mechanical methods like spiking and percussive stunning are among the most practical ways to do it.

Myths about tuna spikes

"Stabbing a fish in the head is cruel."
It looks harsh, but the alternative is usually minutes of suffocation. A spike that ends things in about a second is far kinder.

"It's still moving, so it's still alive."
After a good spike, fish can twitch or flutter their fins. That's leftover nerve activity in the spinal cord, not awareness. (Running a wire down the spinal cord, called shinkei jime, stops it.) A fish that's still conscious will keep swimming, gilling and fighting. If yours is doing that, you missed.

"Ice is the gentle way."
Ice is great for the meat once the fish is dead. On a live fish, it mostly slows things down rather than knocking it out.

"It's only worth it for sushi."
Every fish you keep benefits: better flavor, better texture, longer shelf life, and a safer, calmer deck.

The truth: a missed spike is the inhumane part

A tuna spike is only humane if you hit the brain. A bent screwdriver, a thin ice pick or a spike that's too small for the fish can slip off a thick skull or miss entirely. Then you've hurt the fish without killing it. To do it right:

  1. Use a proper spike, sized to the fish. 1/4" for yellowtail, dorado and school tuna; 5/16" for yellowfin and bluefin; 3/8" for big, thick-skulled fish.
  2. Know where the brain is. Above and slightly behind the eye. On tuna there's a soft spot on top of the head that sits right over it.
  3. Control the fish first. A wet towel over the eyes calms it down.
  4. Push firmly, with a small wiggle. Watch for the shudder and the limp body. If it keeps kicking, reposition and go again right away.
  5. Bleed and ice. Cut the gills, let it bleed in seawater, then into the slurry. See our guides on ike jime and how to bleed a fish.

For small fish like trout, rockfish or bass, a well-aimed blow with a fish priest is a perfectly humane alternative.

FAQ

What is the most humane way to kill a fish?
Destroying the brain immediately, with a brain spike (ike jime) or a hard, accurate blow to the head. Both make the fish unconscious almost instantly.

Is ike jime humane?
Yes, when the spike hits the brain. It's widely considered one of the most humane ways to kill a fish, and it also produces better-tasting meat.

Is putting a fish on ice humane?
Not on its own. Chilling a live fish mostly slows it down rather than knocking it out. Kill the fish first, then ice it.

How long does a fish take to die out of water?
It depends on the species. Research on rainbow trout found it can take roughly 2 to 25 minutes to lose consciousness, and hardy species like carp can last hours.

Why does a fish move after it's been spiked?
That's leftover nerve activity, not awareness. A conscious fish keeps gilling and fighting; a properly spiked fish goes limp, even if it twitches.
