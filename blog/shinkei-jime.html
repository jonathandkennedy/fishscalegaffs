Shinkei jime (神経締め, "nerve killing") is the final, most advanced step of the Japanese ike jime method. After the brain is spiked and the fish is bled, a thin wire is run down the spinal cord to destroy it. That stops the nerves from firing, which keeps the muscle calmer, delays rigor mortis and helps the fish stay fresh longer.

It's what high-end sushi chefs and fish buyers look for. It's also completely optional. Here's what it does, when it's worth it, and how to do it.

Ike jime vs. shinkei jime

People often use the two names interchangeably, but they're different steps:

StepWhat you doTool
Ike jime (brain spike)Pierce the brain to kill the fish instantlyTuna spike / ike jime spike
BleedingCut the gills or behind the pectoral fin and drain the bloodSharp knife, bucket of seawater
Shinkei jimeRun a wire down the spinal cord to destroy the nervesShinkei jime wire
ChillingPut the fish in an ice slurryCrushed ice and seawater

The brain spike, the bleed and the chill do most of the work. Shinkei jime is the extra 10% on top.

Why shinkei jime works

Even after the brain is destroyed, the spinal cord can keep sending signals to the muscles. Those twitches use up the energy stored in the muscle (ATP). Once that energy runs out, the fish goes into rigor mortis and the meat starts to break down.

Destroying the spinal cord stops the twitching. The muscle keeps more of its stored energy, so:

  • Rigor mortis starts later
  • The meat stays firm and clear for longer
  • Flavor and texture hold up over several days, which is why aged sushi fish is often shinkei jime'd

What you need

  • A brain spike. Shinkei jime always starts with a clean ike jime kill. Our Calcutta bamboo tuna spikes are 316 stainless steel in 1/4", 5/16" and 3/8" sizes.
  • Shinkei jime wires. Long, flexible stainless wires (sometimes called "circuit breakers") sized to the fish. Thin wires for small fish, thicker and longer for tuna. The wire needs to be about as long as the fish.
  • A sharp knife for bleeding and, if you go in from the tail, the tail cut.
  • A bucket of seawater and an ice slurry, ready before you start.

How to do shinkei jime, step by step

Step 1: Kill the fish with a brain spike

Push the spike into the brain, above and slightly behind the eye (on tuna, through the soft spot on top of the head). The fish will shudder and go limp. See the ike jime guide for exact placement.

Step 2: Find the spinal canal

The spinal cord runs in a small channel just above the backbone. You can reach it from either end:

  • From the head: go through the hole left by the brain spike, or the nostril on some species, and angle the wire back toward the tail along the top of the backbone.
  • From the tail: cut through the tail wrist just in front of the tail fin. You'll see the backbone with a small hole above it. That's the spinal canal. Push the wire in toward the head.

Step 3: Run the wire

Feed the wire in steadily. When you're in the right channel, it slides with little resistance and the fish will twitch, flutter its fins or flash color as the nerves are destroyed. That's how you know it's working. If the wire stops hard or goes in at a strange angle, back it out and try again.

Push it as far along the spinal cord as you can, then work it back and forth a few times before pulling it out.

Step 4: Bleed and chill

Cut the gills (or behind the pectoral fins on tuna), bleed the fish head-down in seawater, then move it into an ice slurry. Our guide on how to bleed a fish covers this. Many anglers bleed first and run the wire right after. Both orders work, as long as you move quickly.

Is shinkei jime worth it?

  • Worth it: fish you plan to eat raw, dry-age, sell, or keep for several days. Tuna, yellowtail, amberjack, snapper and white seabass all benefit.
  • Not essential: fish you'll cook within a day or two. A clean brain spike, a good bleed and a cold slurry already get you most of the quality.

If you're new to this, master the brain spike and the bleed first. Add shinkei jime once those are second nature.

Common mistakes

  • Skipping the brain spike. Shinkei jime is not a substitute for a clean kill.
  • Using the wrong wire size. Too thin and it kinks; too thick and it won't fit the canal.
  • Going below the backbone. The spinal cord sits above it. If the fish doesn't twitch, you're probably in the wrong spot.
  • Taking too long. All of this works best in the first few minutes after the fish comes on board.

FAQ

What does shinkei jime mean?
"Nerve killing" in Japanese. It's the step where the spinal cord is destroyed with a wire after the fish is killed.

Do you need a special tool for shinkei jime?
Yes, a long, flexible stainless wire sized to the fish. You also need a brain spike for the first step, since shinkei jime always follows a clean ike jime kill.

Is shinkei jime the same as ike jime?
No. Ike jime is the whole method (and often just the brain spike). Shinkei jime is the optional spinal-cord step that comes after.

Which spike should I use before shinkei jime?
Match it to the fish: 1/4" for yellowtail, dorado and school tuna; 5/16" for yellowfin and bluefin; 3/8" for big, thick-skulled fish. You can also design one in your colors with the Custom Tuna Spike Builder.
